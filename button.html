Button

Buttons tell people what they can do, and stand apart from headers and text. The label should make clear what happens when someone presses it.

Usage

  • Use the hierarchy to show how important an action is compared with the actions near it, or with the whole screen.
  • Add an icon, or use an icon-only button, when the action is simple, visual or a common pattern.
  • Use the destructive style for actions like deleting, removing or canceling, and anything else that needs caution or confirmation.

Product usage

  • Moving through flows, such as registration or requesting a visit
  • Submitting forms
  • Homepage tiles

Used in

  • Header (a modified version)
  • Inline notification
  • Modal
  • Table
  • Toast notification

Variants and options

Placement

Forms

One button

  • Large screens: a primary button at the bottom of the form, left-aligned with it.
  • Small screens: a primary button at the bottom of the form, or a fixed ("sticky") action footer. The button can fill the width of the content.

Several buttons

  • Use a primary button for the main action, such as Save, and group the buttons in a row at the bottom of the form, left-aligned with it.
  • On small screens, stack them if needed, with the primary button first.

Multi-step flows

Place the flow's navigation so it reads like a book: in left-to-right languages, you turn pages from right to left to move forward. Group the actions at the bottom of the content. Right-align the primary button for moving forward (usually Next), and left-align a less prominent button for going back (usually Back).

Modals

Large screens

  • The primary button sits farthest right, aligned with the modal's content.
  • A secondary button sits to its left, in a right-aligned group.
  • A tertiary button (usually Cancel) sits farthest left, aligned with the content.

Small screens

  • Stack them: primary first, then secondary, then tertiary (usually Cancel) last.

Interaction

Tap targets

Dashed outlines show the tap target.

  • Primary and secondary buttons: the tap target is the button.
  • Tertiary buttons: the tap target is bigger than the button looks, to reach the 44 × 44px minimum.

Platforms

  • iOS and Android buttons have the same states as web, since phones and tablets can use a mouse or keyboard.
  • Figma handoff usually shows only the default and pressed states.

Accessibility

Using a button on a page still needs an annotated design review of the page-level design.

Web

  • Buttons are reached with Tab and pressed with Space or Enter.
  • Icons are decorative: give them empty alt text (alt="").
  • A button can have the link or button role, depending on what it does. In code, pass href to render a link.

iOS and Android

  • People reach buttons by swiping left or right with VoiceOver on.
  • VoiceOver reads "Label, button", such as "Next, button".
  • Disabled, view-only and loading buttons read "Label, dimmed, button", such as "Confirm, dimmed, button".
  • Icons are decorative and have no alt text.

Content guidelines

  • Lead with a verb, not a noun: "View more info", not "More info".
  • Use sentence case and no end punctuation.
  • Be brief: four words or fewer, without filler like "the". Don't give up clarity for brevity; writers can make exceptions.
  • Say what happens next when you can. Avoid vague labels like "Learn more" without context.
  • Don't repeat the header or body copy around it.

Code vs. Figma.
  • The React loading state uses the Pulse spinner everywhere. On iOS, Figma uses the native loader.
  • Placement, such as sticky action footers, book-style flow navigation and modal action bars, is up to the page. The Do and Don't examples here are built from plain buttons.
